## Why we get nervous

Your body treats being watched and judged as a threat: heart beats faster, hands shake, voice trembles, the mind goes blank. This is the same "fight or flight" reaction that helped humans survive. It is **normal**, even for experienced speakers.

The good news: **the audience sees much less than you feel.** Studies call this the "spotlight effect": people notice far less of our nervousness than we imagine, because they are busy thinking about themselves.

| What you feel | What the audience sees |
| --- | --- |
| "My hands are shaking badly" | usually nothing |
| "My voice sounds weak" | a slightly quiet voice |
| "I forgot a line, everyone noticed" | a short pause (which sounds thoughtful) |
| "I made a mistake, I look stupid" | a mistake they forget in a minute |

## Before you speak: calm the body

| Technique | How | Why it works |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Slow breathing** | breathe in for 4 counts, hold for 2, out for 6; repeat 5 times | slows the heart, calms the nerves |
| **Move** | walk, stretch your shoulders, shake out your hands | burns off nervous energy |
| **Drink water** | a few sips before and a glass nearby | dry mouth is common |
| **Power pose** | stand tall, shoulders back, for a minute in private | helps you feel steadier |
| **Arrive early** | see the room, test the equipment | removes surprises |
| **Reframe** | tell yourself "I am excited" instead of "I am scared" | the body feels almost the same; the meaning is better |

## Prepare in the right way

The strongest cure for fear is **knowing your material and having practised aloud**.

1. **Know your first minute by heart.** The opening is where nerves are worst. Learn the first two or three sentences word for word.
2. **Know your last minute too.** A strong ending is easy to deliver and sticks in memory.
3. **Know the middle by points, not by words.** Memorising everything word for word makes you freeze if you forget one line. Learn 3 to 5 key points and the order.
4. **Practise aloud**, standing up, with a timer. Reading silently does not count.
5. **Practise in front of a person** or record a video on your phone and watch it (it is uncomfortable, and it works).

## A daily practice routine

Confidence is a **skill**, trained in small steps.

| Level | Practice | Time |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | read a news paragraph aloud, slowly and clearly | 5 minutes a day |
| 2 | talk about your day to a mirror or phone camera for one minute | 5 minutes |
| 3 | explain a topic you learned to a friend, as if teaching | 10 minutes |
| 4 | answer one question out loud in class or a meeting | each week |
| 5 | give a 3-minute talk to a small group | each month |

Look for small chances to speak: ask a question in class, volunteer to read, introduce a speaker, give a vote of thanks, join a club or a debate.

## Handling mistakes

| If you... | Do this |
| --- | --- |
| forget what to say | pause, take a breath, look at your notes, continue (a pause feels longer to you than to them) |
| stumble over a word | carry on; do not apologise repeatedly |
| lose your place | say, "Let me come back to the main point", and move on |
| get a question you cannot answer | say, "That is a good question; I do not have the figure now, and I will find out and tell you" |
| notice you are speaking too fast | pause and take a breath at the end of the next sentence |

:::remember
Nobody remembers a speaker who made one small mistake. They remember a speaker who was **clear, honest and helpful**. Aim for connection, not perfection.
:::
