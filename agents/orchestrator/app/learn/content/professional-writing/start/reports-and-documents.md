## Why structure matters

People rarely read documents word by word. They **scan**: the title, the first lines, the headings, the bullets, the numbers. Good structure lets a manager understand your report in a minute, and find details later.

## A reliable structure for almost any report

| Section | Purpose | Length |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Title** | what the document is | one line |
| **Summary** (or "Key points") | the conclusion and the main numbers | 3 to 5 lines |
| **Background** | why this matters, only what the reader needs | short |
| **Findings or details** | the evidence, in sections with headings | the bulk |
| **Recommendation or next steps** | what to do, who does it, by when | short, specific |
| **Attachments** | tables, links, long data | as needed |

This is called the **inverted pyramid**: the most important information goes at the top, details below.

## Example: a weekly project update

:::example Weekly update (clear and scannable)
**Project: Student Portal. Week ending 8 March**

**Summary**
The login module is complete and tested. Payment integration is two days behind schedule because of delays in receiving the gateway keys. We expect to finish it by 14 March.

**Done this week**
- Login and password reset: completed and tested
- 14 of 15 bugs from the last test round fixed

**In progress**
- Payment integration: 60% complete

**Risks**
- Gateway keys not yet received. *Owner: Meena. Chased on 6 March; next follow-up 10 March.*

**Next week**
- Complete payment integration (by 14 March)
- Start user testing with 10 students
:::

Everything a manager needs is visible in ten lines.

## Paragraphs and headings

| Good practice | Why |
| --- | --- |
| one idea per paragraph, 3 to 5 lines | easier to read on a screen |
| a clear heading for each section | readers find what they need |
| bullets for lists of three or more items | faster to scan than a long sentence |
| a table for comparisons | shows differences at a glance |
| **bold** only for the few things that matter | too much bold stops working |
| the same style for dates, numbers and names throughout | looks careful and professional |

## Use numbers, not adjectives

| Vague | Precise |
| --- | --- |
| "Sales improved a lot." | "Sales rose 18%, from ₹4.2 lakh to ₹4.96 lakh." |
| "The site is slow." | "The home page takes 6 seconds to load; the target is 2." |
| "Many students attended." | "142 of 180 students (79%) attended." |
| "We need more time." | "We need 3 more days to finish testing." |

Always say what a number is **compared with** (last month, the target, the average).

## Writing a short report in four steps

1. **Decide the main point** in one sentence. Write it as the summary first.
2. **List the evidence** (three to five points) that supports it.
3. **Add the next step**: what do you want the reader to do?
4. **Cut**: remove everything that does not help the reader decide or act.

## Meeting notes (minutes)

Short and useful minutes are loved by teams:

| Section | Content |
| --- | --- |
| Date, attendees | who was there |
| **Decisions** | what was agreed |
| **Action items** | task, owner, deadline |
| Open questions | what is not yet decided |

:::example Action items
| Task | Owner | Due |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Share the draft design | Arun | 12 March |
| Confirm the venue booking | Meena | 10 March |
| Prepare the budget sheet | Karthik | 14 March |
:::

## Common problems

| Problem | Fix |
| --- | --- |
| the main point is hidden on page 3 | move the conclusion to the top |
| long sentences with many "which" and "that" | split them |
| passive, vague wording ("it was decided that...") | say who: "The team decided..." |
| no clear next step | end with who does what by when |
| inconsistent formatting | use the same headings and styles |

:::tip
When you finish writing, ask: "If the reader only reads the first five lines, will they know what I want them to know?" If not, rewrite the top.
:::
